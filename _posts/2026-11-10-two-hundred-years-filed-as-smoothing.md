The most quoted number in paleoclimate is a decade. Somewhere in the last glacial period, every one or two thousand years, Greenland warmed by something between 5 and 16 °C, and the ice cores say it happened in about as long as it takes a child to finish primary school. That number is why "abrupt climate change" is a phrase at all. It is the empirical anchor under every tipping-point conversation: the planet has done this before, fast, and the evidence is layered in the ice at Summit and NGRIP in annual bands you can count.

A [paper in *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aed4270) last week says the decade is real and the thing it measures is not what everyone thought. Xiyu Dong and twenty-four co-authors — including, notably, several of the Copenhagen and Oregon State ice-core people whose own archive is being demoted here — dated the same Dansgaard–Oeschger warmings in cave calcite instead of ice, and got durations that start above a century and average about 180 years.

## The dating inversion

What makes this more than another proxy squabble is which kind of precision each archive has.

Ice cores are superb at relative time and mediocre at absolute time. You count layers, so you know that this event took 43 years rather than 310, but the whole counted column floats: errors accumulate downward, and by 40,000 years ago the absolute age carries centuries of slack. Speleothems are the exact mirror. Uranium–thorium dating pins a stalagmite's absolute age with real precision, but it gives you dated points, not a clock between them, and growth rate can swing by a factor of several. Dong puts it plainly in the press release: U–Th dating gives precise absolute ages, but it cannot give reliable relative ages.

Which is why the two stalagmites at the centre of this paper matter — one from Cherrapunji Cave in northeast India, one from Shuixi Cave in southwest China, both annually laminated. Visible yearly bands turn a cave into the thing a cave normally isn't: a layer-counted archive. With published records from Zhangjia Cave, that gave the team a relative chronology good enough to time the duration of each of the eight warmings from DO-1 to DO-8, and every one of them took more than a hundred years.

## Two stages, one of them invisible

The interpretation the paper builds, with isotope-enabled climate models doing the translating, is that a DO warming has two stages and Greenland only records the first.

Stage one: sea ice retreats in the northeastern North Atlantic. The regional atmosphere reorganises — storm tracks, moisture pathways, the whole circulation regime over the Nordic Seas flips from stadial to interstadial. Greenland's δ18O jumps, because δ18O over Greenland is sensitive to exactly that: where its snow came from and how the air got there.

Stage two: the Atlantic overturning keeps strengthening, sea ice in the *northwestern* North Atlantic keeps retreating, and the hemisphere keeps adjusting — for another century and a half. But the moisture pathways feeding Greenland have already switched. There is no further step in δ18O to record, so the ice goes quiet while the ocean is still moving.

The Asian monsoon δ18O, on this reading, tracks the whole thing, because it responds to the full ocean-driven reorganisation rather than to one local switch.

## The headline overshoots in a specific direction

The press release says ice-age abrupt climate shifts were not as fast as thought. That is the wrong summary of the paper's own claim, and the difference is not pedantic.

It is also worth saying that the ice-core literature never held the decade as tightly as the phrase suggests. [Capron and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-22241-w) fitted ramps to every transition in NEEM and NGRIP in 2021 and found 19 of 24 and 20 of 25 respectively faster than a century — but with duration uncertainty intervals averaging about 117 years and running from 2 to 288. The headline decade comes from the fastest-switching tracer in the fastest-resolved events; the fitted range always had centuries in it.

Greenland's shifts *were* that fast. The deuterium excess — the moisture-source fingerprint — switched mode in one to three years at the ends of the Younger Dryas and the preceding stadial, with implied source-region temperature changes of 2 to 4 K from one year to the next. Nothing in the new work touches that. Nor does it touch the magnitudes: the nitrogen-isotope gas thermometer in NGRIP gives abrupt warmings from 5 °C to 16.5 °C with ±3 °C uncertainty, typically 9 to 13 °C, and the speleothems say nothing about how big the temperature jumps were.

What the paper claims is narrower and more interesting: *the ocean circulation* took longer than the Greenland signal implies. Abrupt regional climate change is intact. The inference from it to abrupt AMOC change is what is being cut.

That matters because the inference is load-bearing. The reason a glacial ocean event is in the modern tipping-point conversation at all is the claim that overturning can reorganise on a human timescale. Statistical early-warning work on the modern AMOC, most prominently [Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen's 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-39810-w) estimate of a tipping point centred on 2057 with a 95% range of 2025 to 2095, is about *when* a threshold is crossed and is explicitly silent on how long the transition then takes. The glacial record has been doing that second job by analogy. If the analogy says 180 years rather than 10, the shape of the risk changes — not the existence of it.

## Where this is thinner than it sounds

I can see the abstract, the author list and the press release; the paper itself is behind Science's paywall, so I have not read the per-event durations, the uncertainty on that 180-year mean, or the model configuration. Several things I would want to check are therefore unchecked, and a few of them look load-bearing.

**The proxy doing the convicting has its own open case.** Asian monsoon speleothem δ18O is one of the most argued-about proxies in the field. [Maher and Thompson's 2012](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/53635) mass-balance argument is that Chinese cave δ18O records circulation regime and moisture source, not rainfall amount, and that the standard reading implies Holocene and glacial rainfall swings other proxies flatly don't show. The new paper's conclusion is that ice δ18O is a partial recorder and cave δ18O is a complete one. Both halves of that come from isotope-enabled models simulating both archives correctly. That isn't circular, but it is model-mediated at the exact joint carrying the weight.

**Eight events, three caves.** "Longer than a century in every case" is a strong statement from a sample this size, and the mean of 180 years is a mean over eight numbers whose individual error bars I cannot see. Lamina counting in calcite has its own failure modes — growth-rate changes, hiatuses, sub-annual banding — and a 100-versus-180-year distinction lives entirely inside that error budget.

**The glacial-to-modern transfer is the weakest step and nobody's paper is responsible for it.** The stage-one mechanism here is sea-ice retreat in a Nordic Seas that was mostly ice-covered, under a glacial atmosphere with Northern Hemisphere ice sheets. There is no clean modern analogue, which is a reason to be careful about reading 180 years off this record and onto a twenty-first-century forecast — including when the reading makes the news less alarming.

## The number that was already there

The detail I keep turning over is in the bar chart above, and it is twelve years old.

[Kindler and colleagues](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/10/887/2014/) reconstructed NGRIP temperature in 2014 from δ15N in trapped air — a thermometer physically independent of δ18O, since it reads firn thickness and thermal fractionation rather than moisture history. For DO-8, their record gives a warming that took roughly 200 years. They flagged it as probably too long and said why: δ15N resolution is lower, their input was smoothed with a 200-year spline, and gas signals get smeared during bubble close-off. The high-resolution δ18O, they noted, put DO-8 at about 21 years.

So an independent ice-core thermometer said two centuries, and the field read the discrepancy as an artifact of the thermometer. Which may well be exactly what it was — firn smoothing is real physics, and it would broaden a genuinely 21-year transition into something like what they saw. The ~200-year figure is consistent with a fast transition badly resolved *and* with a slow one well resolved, so it corroborates nothing on its own. It is a coincidence, not evidence.

But it is the kind of coincidence worth noticing, because of what the resolution of the disagreement assumed. Two instruments disagreed, and the slower one's answer was attributed to its own blurring — with δ18O treated as the standard that the other proxy had to be reconciled to. That is a defensible methodological choice and it was argued for explicitly. It is also precisely the choice the new paper says was the wrong way round.

If Dong et al. are right, there is a sharp consequence: during stage two, when δ18O has gone flat, Greenland temperature should still be climbing. The δ15N thermometer reads temperature, not moisture pathway, so it should see the slow half. That is my inference and not theirs, but it is testable with ice already drilled, and it does not depend on cave calcite or on any monsoon isotope model being right. A high-resolution δ15N profile across a couple of these transitions, with the smoothing forward-modelled rather than assumed, would settle whether the slow stage is in the ice and was being read out.

The thing I would most want, though, is a direct rate constraint on the circulation itself at century resolution for the same events — a Pa/Th or sortable-silt record fine enough to time stage two in the ocean rather than inferring it from isotopes in a cave two continents away. Until something like that exists, the strongest version of this result is not "the AMOC took 180 years." It is "the decade everyone quotes was never a measurement of the AMOC," which is a smaller claim, better supported, and the part that actually changes how the old record should be used.
