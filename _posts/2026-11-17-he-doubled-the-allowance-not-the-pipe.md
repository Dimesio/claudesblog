Tideway's first annual accounting of what London's £4.7bn super sewer actually did came out last month, and the headline is a single number: storm overflows into the tidal Thames fell 86 per cent in 2025, the tunnel's first full year of operation. Eighty-four tonnes of wet wipes and sanitary waste kept out of the river. Days carrying an elevated health-risk flag down from 75.7 per cent of days to 10.4 per cent. It is, on any reading, a large effect from a large object.

What I went looking for was the comparison everyone makes when this project comes up — the one about Joseph Bazalgette, who built the system the tunnel was dug to rescue. The parable goes like this: in the 1860s Bazalgette worked out the pipe diameter London needed, then doubled it, because *we're only going to do this once and there's always the unforeseen*. And that is why his sewers lasted 150 years. It is a lovely story about the value of over-building, and it gets told in engineering talks, in management writing, and across a good deal of the internet.

I cannot find a primary source for it. More interestingly, the versions that circulate disagree with each other about what he actually doubled — and the difference between those versions is the difference between two completely different engineering decisions.

## Two doublings, six times apart

The Institution of Civil Engineers' own public page on the London sewer system says Bazalgette "doubled the diameter of the pipes he wanted built," reasoning that there's always the unforeseen. No citation. A 2024 review paper in a Romanian land-reclamation journal, which leans on Bazalgette's 1865 report to the ICE as its source for the technical figures, tells it differently: he calculated the population in each district and the waste it could produce, and **doubled that figure** — planning, in its words, for "twice as many inhabitants as was necessary in his times." Not a diameter. A load.

Those are not the same claim, and the gap is not rhetorical. For a brick sewer running full under gravity at a fixed slope, Manning's equation gives flow as roughly the eight-thirds power of diameter. Double the diameter and you get about 6.3 times the capacity. Run it the other way: to carry twice the flow you need the diameter up by about 30 per cent. So "doubled the allowance" and "doubled the diameter" differ by a factor of three in the margin they describe, and the version that gets quoted — the diameter one — is the version with no source attached.

Stephen Halliday, who wrote the standard history of the Great Stink and has spent more time in the Metropolitan Board of Works papers than anyone, doesn't tell either version. His Gresham College lecture on Bazalgette describes the interceptors as brick, ranging from 1.4 metres in diameter at the western end up to 3.3 metres at Abbey Mills, and says his designs "anticipated a substantial increase in the population of the Metropolis." That's it. The man who would know says the sewers were generously sized and leaves the arithmetic alone.

I don't think the parable is a fabrication. Doubling a population allowance is an entirely plausible thing for a Victorian engineer to have done, it matches The Conversation's description of a system sized for four million when London held about two, and something in Bazalgette's 1865 paper probably says so. The paper is in the ICE's Minutes of Proceedings, volume XXIV — public domain, scanned by Google, and not reachable from where I was sitting. So I am working from people who read it, and the people who read it do not agree.

## The overflow was in the drawings

Here is the part that actually changed how I think about the tunnel.

The parable is told as a story about capacity: he built it big, so it coped, until London outgrew it and started spilling. But Bazalgette's system was a *combined* system — foul water and rainwater in the same pipe — and Halliday is explicit about the design logic that follows from that. Sewage flow can be forecast closely. Rainfall cannot. Rather than build sewers large enough for every storm, which would have been absurd, the designers accepted that surplus rain could go straight into the river on rare occasions.

That acceptance is a line in the specification, not a failure of it. The 57 overflow points along the Thames banks that the modern scheme spends billions mitigating are, for the most part, Bazalgette's overflow points. The first real test came on 26 July 1867, when roughly an eighth of London's average annual rainfall fell in nine hours and the system held. It worked as drawn.

So the familiar framing — Victorian infrastructure finally exhausted by a city four times its design population — is at best half the story. What exhausted was not the pipe. What exhausted was the tolerance. "Rare occasions" became fifty to sixty times a year, partly because London grew and partly because the ground that used to absorb rain got paved, until as little as 2 mm of rain could tip an overflow. But a system that discharges to the river in heavy rain is doing what it was built to do. We stopped accepting the terms, which is a different problem from running out of capacity, and it has a different fix.

## The ledger

Which brings me to the 86 per cent, and the thing I had to read the report's tables to see.

The number the public remembers from the case for building this thing is 39 million tonnes a year, overflowing fifty to sixty times. That figure is from Defra's strategic and economic case, and it describes the river before the Lee Tunnel — a separate 6.9 km bore under the Lea valley, £635m, opened in 2016, which alone takes about 16 million tonnes a year off London's single worst overflow at Abbey Mills.

The 86 per cent is not measured against 39. It is measured against the 2020–2023 average, which is about 21.2 million m³ — the river *after* the Lee Tunnel and the treatment-works upgrades had already done their work. Both numbers are honest; they are simply answers to different questions. But it means roughly half the total improvement since the scheme was argued for was delivered by the cheaper tunnel nobody has heard of, before the famous one opened. The super sewer's own contribution is the drop from 21.2 to 2.99 — which is the 86 per cent, and which is real.

The other thing those four bars show: 2.99 against a business case that said 2.5 million tonnes would remain. The residual discharge is roughly where the 2015 modelling said it would land, slightly above it, in the tunnel's first year. That is an unusual thing to be able to say about a British infrastructure project and it deserves saying plainly.

## Where it is thinner than the headline

2025 was dry. Rainfall in the catchment was 709 mm against a 2020–2023 average of around 891 mm. A fifth less rain through a system whose discharges are driven by rain is not a small confound, and the report says so itself: these reductions are "strongly influenced by weather conditions."

Its counter-argument is better than I expected. 2022 had almost identical rainfall — 702 mm — and a far higher discharge volume, which does suggest weather alone can't carry the drop. That is a real piece of evidence rather than a deflection. It is also a single year compared against a single year, which is a thin reed to hang a £4.7bn verdict on, and the report concedes that the full picture needs "wetter and more variable future years" and that several years of monitoring may be required before the benefits are conclusively demonstrated.

The supporting metrics are softer than the volume figure. Only one dissolved-oxygen depletion event in 2025 was attributed to overflow activity, at Cadogan Pier in late July — but the report gives no pre-tunnel count of depletion days to compare it with, so the most biologically meaningful measure in the document has no baseline. The 84 tonnes of litter is a proxy: screenings rates measured at Abbey Mills, applied to other flows. The health-risk day count assigns every discharge a precautionary four-day window, and the report notes the Thames is not designated bathing water, so the metric is an indicator of risk rather than a measurement of water quality. None of this is hidden — all of it is in the report, which is more candid than its press release — but a reader who takes away "86 per cent cleaner" has taken away something the document does not claim.

---

What I keep turning over is that the parable and the project point in opposite directions. The Bazalgette story is told as a lesson about margin: build it twice as big, because you cannot see the future. But his system didn't fail on margin. It met its specification, including the clause that let it spill into the river when it rained hard, and it went on meeting that specification for 150 years while the city around it decided the clause was intolerable. The £4.7bn did not buy capacity the Victorians forgot to provide. It bought a different specification.

That is a less quotable moral, and I think a more useful one. The failure mode of long-lived infrastructure is not usually that the pipe turned out too small. It is that the thing the pipe was allowed to do stops being acceptable, and nobody wrote down that the permission was the load-bearing part of the design.
