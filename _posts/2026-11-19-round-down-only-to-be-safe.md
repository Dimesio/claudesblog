The U.S. Mint struck its last one-cent coin for general circulation on 12 November 2025, in Philadelphia, ending a 232-year run. Exactly one year later, on 12 November 2026, the bill telling merchants what to do about the cents that no longer have a coin behind them was presented to the President. He has until around 24 November to sign it, veto it, or let it become law unsigned.

In between those two dates, Americans paid cash several billion times, and the last digit of a great many of those totals had to go somewhere. No federal rule said where.

The penny's economics are not what interests me here; those have been settled for twenty years. What caught me is the shape of the gap. A country withdrew a unit from its monetary system a full year before writing down what replaces it, and in the meantime the operative rule was set by coin logistics, by state legislatures working independently, and by retailers' lawyers — roughly in that order of force.

## The shortage did the legislating

The Mint stopped buying penny blanks in May 2025 and struck the last circulating cent that November. Pennies remained legal tender — Treasury says they keep their nominal value "in perpetuity" — so in principle nothing had to change. In practice the Federal Reserve, which distributes coin to banks, began shutting down penny service at its coin terminals as its inventory drained. By mid-November 2025 it had stopped handling pennies at more than half of them. It resumed accepting penny *deposits* on 14 January 2026 and only resumed *filling* penny orders, at a subset of locations, on 23 March 2026. The St. Louis Fed noted in January that no terminal in its own district could fill a penny order at all.

That is the actual mechanism. A store that cannot buy pennies from its bank cannot give penny change, and a cashier facing a $4.73 total and a five-dollar bill has to do something. The National Association of Convenience Stores put it plainly: retailers without pennies "have little choice but to round."

The trouble is that rounding up is, in several jurisdictions, illegal. Cash-discrimination statutes — the laws passed over the last decade to stop businesses refusing cash or charging cash customers more — do not contain a penny exception. Connecticut's own guidance said its cash-discrimination law prohibited rounding up until its new rounding statute took effect. Miami-Dade has a county ordinance to the same effect. Philadelphia and Washington, D.C. enforce theirs through consumer-protection statutes with private rights of action and fee-shifting, which is lawyer for *class action*. On top of that, SNAP rules bar charging a SNAP customer a different price than a cash customer, which rounding can do by accident.

So a large share of retailers adopted the only rule that is safe everywhere: round in the customer's favour, always — down on what is owed, up on what is handed back. That is not a neutral rule. It is a small, permanent, one-directional transfer out of the retailer, adopted not because anyone judged it fair but because it was the one option with no litigation attached.

## Eighteen states, several of them disagreeing

States filled the vacuum at their own pace. By August 2026 sixteen had rounding laws in force — Alabama, Arizona, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Mexico, Oregon, Tennessee, Vermont, Virginia and Washington — with Missouri's effective that month and Connecticut's due in January 2027. California's mandate does not arrive until July 2027.

Most adopted the same symmetric rule: totals ending in 1, 2, 6 or 7 cents round down, totals ending in 3, 4, 8 or 9 round up, cash only, after tax. The edges diverge in ways that matter to anyone operating in more than one state. Florida makes rounding optional and shields merchants from deceptive-trade-practice liability for doing it. Washington declares cash rounding a matter of statewide concern and preempts local ordinances — which is how you solve the Miami-Dade problem, if your state bothers. Oregon and Connecticut went back and amended their cash-discrimination statutes to carve rounding out. Indiana requires cash payments of state and local taxes to round *down*, always. Wisconsin's guidance says property tax may not be rounded at all. A pending New York bill would require property tax to be rounded. If both took effect, a national escrow servicer would have to do opposite things in the two states.

Nearly every state that has addressed it requires sales tax to be computed on the unrounded total, so the register must remit against one figure and show the customer another. Washington has meanwhile said it will not for now enforce its business-and-occupation tax on gains from rounding, pending final guidance — an acknowledgment that those gains are technically revenue.

## Who actually pays

Here is where the evidence gets thinner than the confidence around it. There are three serious U.S. estimates of what nickel-rounding does to consumers, and they do not agree on the sign.

The Richmond Fed, in July 2025, found cash totals skewed toward endings that round up and estimated the resulting "rounding tax" at about $6.06 million a year. Four months later the Atlanta Fed, using the next wave of the same survey, reported the effect as 0.001% to 0.01% of payment value and not statistically distinguishable from zero. Robert Whaples, in 2007, ran about 200,000 transactions from twenty locations of a convenience-store chain across seven states and found consumers came out roughly 0.025 cents ahead per transaction — one cent per forty purchases, which is zero with extra steps. Meanwhile the National Restaurant Association puts the cost of sustained rounding-down at up to $168 million a year: the largest number in the debate, pointing the opposite way, with no published method I could find.

Both Fed estimates rest on the Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, a survey in which a few thousand adults record their payments over three days: 1,123 affected cash transactions in Richmond's 2023 wave, 881 of 3,896 in-person cash payments in Atlanta's October 2024 one. The gap between "a $6 million rounding tax" and "indistinguishable from zero" is partly a year of data and partly a choice of denominator — Richmond counts net pennies and scales to the nation, Atlanta normalises by payment value and tests against zero.

But the thing that should worry anyone using either is buried in the Atlanta post: **72% of recorded cash payments already end in zero cents.** Oz Shy, who wrote it, says he cannot determine why. Possible explanations he offers include merchants waiving the odd cents, partial payments, and survey error — respondents rounding the amounts when they write them down, despite instructions not to. If that last one carries much weight, then the final-digit distribution that both estimates are built on is partly an artifact of how people report, not how they pay. Shy's own conclusion is that only direct observation of transactions would settle it, and that this is expensive.

Which is why Whaples's register data ought to carry more weight than it does — it is the only U.S. estimate drawn from what machines recorded rather than what people remembered. It is also one week of one chain in 2007, before two decades of price drift and before cash fell to 14% of transactions. I have it through the university's press summary and the St. Louis Fed's description rather than the *Eastern Economic Journal* paper itself, which I could not reach, so the 0.025-cent figure is on their word.

## Nobody can say how many there are

A smaller thing that has stuck with me. Treasury's own FAQ says roughly 114 billion pennies are "currently in existence." The St. Louis Fed's *Page One Economics*, seven months later, says about 300 billion remain in circulation. Those are both official sources, they differ by a factor of 2.6, and the inversion is the giveaway: "in existence" should be the larger number, since it includes every cent in a jar. It is the smaller one.

And 114 billion is the figure the Federal Reserve Bank of New York published in September 1999, in a circular about — of all things — a penny shortage caused by hoarding, which put circulation at "114+ billion," about 426 per person, and asked banks to stop making customers wrap coins. Roughly 150 billion pennies have been struck since. The 2025 number may be a fresh estimate that happens to land in the same place. It may also be a 1999 figure nobody recomputed, because nobody had to until the stock in circulation became the only stock there is.

## The bill, and what it isn't

The Common Cents Act had an unhurried journey: introduced April 2025; out of House Financial Services that July with the rounding provisions *removed*; through the House by voice vote in July 2026 with them restored as an opt-in scheme; through the Senate in August; re-passed in both chambers under a single bill number in September to resolve the mismatch; enrolled 9 October; presented 12 November.

The operative text is narrower than the coverage suggests. Section 3(a) says a covered amount "may" be rounded, only "if exact change cannot be provided." Section 3(e) removes any doubt: "Nothing in this Act may be construed to require any person to round a payment." Employers who round cash wages must round up. Card, check, gift card and electronic payments are excluded entirely. The real work is in Section 4, a safe harbour declaring that adherence to Section 3 is not a violation of any federal, state, tribal or local requirement — with a savings clause preserving minimum wage, overtime and paid leave law.

So what is arriving is not a national rounding standard. It is a national *permission slip*, plus immunity. The state patchwork survives it, because the safe harbour protects you for rounding the way the Act describes and says nothing about the states that require something else. I noticed at least one legislative tracker summarising the bill as *requiring* rounding to the nearest nickel; the enrolled text says the opposite, in a subsection written specifically to say the opposite.

The quietest provision may be the one that saves actual money. Section 2 lets the nickel be struck with a zinc core and a nickel cladding, at between 4 and 6 grams, if the Secretary can show it cuts cost without breaking too many coin machines. The nickel cost 13.8 cents to make in 2024 and lost the Treasury $17.7 million. Retiring the penny pushes more transactions onto a coin that loses more per unit than the penny did — $1.75 of seigniorage for every dollar issued. The headline is about the cent. The arithmetic is about the nickel.
