Last October, Verra published a one-page notice reminding anyone building a soil-carbon project under its agricultural land management methodology that their soil data has to reach at least thirty centimetres down. Projects had been arriving with shallower numbers lifted from national soil surveys, and the notice shut that door: thirty centimetres for model inputs, thirty centimetres for measured values, and anything shallower is good only for calibration, with the extrapolation documented. The justification offered is the IPCC's — the top thirty centimetres is the layer land management actually reaches.

As enforcement, it is enforcement, and the direction is right. But thirty centimetres is also, in practice, where everybody stops. It is the floor and the ceiling at once. And nearly every serious objection to paying farmers for soil carbon turns out to be an objection about what happens just underneath that line — or about something stranger, which is that the quantity being sold and the quantity being measured do not have the same units.

## The carbon that moved instead of arriving

The claim is simple enough to print on a seed bag: stop ploughing, and carbon accumulates in your soil. The measurements under it are real. A no-till field almost always holds more organic carbon in its top five to ten centimetres than the ploughed field beside it. Residue stays on the surface instead of being folded in, the surface runs cooler and wetter, and carbon builds up where you would expect.

The objection, which is nearly twenty years old, is that the comparison is being made in the wrong place. In 2007 John Baker and colleagues published a commentary in *Agriculture, Ecosystems and Environment* noting that of the 140 tillage experiments in West and Post's heavily cited 2002 review, **none sampled below thirty centimetres** — while maize roots, as a root-excavation study from 1922 had already established, go a great deal deeper than that.

Their summary of a Canadian dataset is the number that stuck with me. Where sampling stopped at thirty centimetres or shallower, 37 of 45 no-till treatments held more carbon than their tilled pairs, averaging a gain of 0.38 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year. Where sampling went deeper, 35 of 51 held *less* carbon, averaging a loss of 0.23. The same practice, the same country, opposite signs, and the thing that flipped the sign was how far down the auger went.

If that pattern holds, what no-till does is not accumulate carbon so much as move it upward. Powlson and Jenkinson reached the same conclusion about ploughed and unploughed cereal plots at Rothamsted in 1981, measuring to forty centimetres: little effect on soil organic matter other than altering its distribution through the profile. They did it on an equivalent mass basis, a phrase that turns out to be the whole second half of this story.

The most careful modern version I could reach is Ogle and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* in 2019: 178 experimental sites, 1,205 observations, and — the methodological choice that makes it useful — carbon change modelled as a continuous function of depth rather than lumped into whatever intervals each original study happened to use. Their crossover sits around twenty centimetres. Above it, no-till holds more carbon; below it, full tillage does. Twenty centimetres is *inside* the plough layer, not beneath it.

What that paper does not say is that no-till never works. It finds genuine gains in particular places: about 0.54 t C/ha/yr in loamy to clayey soils in tropical moist and wet climates (95% CI 0.04–1.02), about 0.5 in sandy soils in warm temperate moist climates (0.24–0.76). In cool and warm temperate dry climates the means are positive and the confidence intervals swallow zero. So the honest summary is not "no-till doesn't sequester carbon." It is that no-till sequesters carbon in some soils and climates, by an amount whose uncertainty often includes nothing at all, and you cannot tell which case you are standing in by sampling to thirty centimetres.

## The part that has nothing to do with depth

Here is the bit I had not properly understood until today, and it is why I wanted to write this up.

A soil carbon stock is a *mass*: percent organic carbon multiplied by the mass of soil it sits in. But a soil core is a *volume*. You push a tube thirty centimetres into the ground and you have a fixed depth of soil whose mass depends on how tightly it is packed. Loosen a field and the same carbon occupies a taller column, so some of it is now below your tube. Compact a field and you have pulled carbon up into your tube from deeper down. Neither case involves any carbon entering or leaving the field.

This is not a hypothetical. The plough layer under no-till runs roughly two to three percent denser than the plough layer of a tilled field — a difference CarbonPlan flagged in 2021 as large enough to bias fixed-depth stock estimates on its own. And the reverse happens too: Jonathan Sanderman, writing to the Climate Action Reserve in August 2020 during the public comment period on its Soil Enrichment Protocol, pointed out that improved management often *fluffs* soil up, so a project that genuinely builds carbon can measure a loss. His formulation is the clearest I have read:

> SOC stock is OC% × soil mass, you are 'losing' soil mass with some SOC in it by always sampling to the same depth.

He also asked the obvious question that a protocol mentioning bulk density conversions but not requiring bulk density measurement invites: how do you convert from concentration to stock without measuring bulk density? His recommendations were sampling to at least forty centimetres in two or more increments, real bulk density measurement, and reporting on an equivalent-mass basis.

How big is the effect? Von Haden, Bernacchi and colleagues ran the experiment the cheap way in *Global Change Biology* in 2020: take real soil data from four bioenergy cropping systems in central Illinois, simulate bulk density changes on top of it, and see which accounting method recovers the truth. With 2.5 cm of simulated compaction or expansion, fixed-depth accounting produced a mean absolute error of 23.2% in the top ten centimetres; equivalent soil mass produced 0.2%. Across depth intervals, fixed depth was three to a hundred and two times worse. Even half a centimetre of movement pushed fixed-depth error above 4% while equivalent mass stayed under 0.1%. They note that surface bulk density changes of five to twenty percent are routinely observed after a change in land use — an order of magnitude larger than the perturbations where fixed depth already falls apart.

CarbonPlan tried to scale this up across US cropland using SSURGO soil maps and CropScape, and reported that in their medium scenario, failing to correct for density change under thirty-centimetre fixed-depth sampling would over-report true carbon gains by 367 million tonnes of CO₂ — about 58% of US agricultural sector emissions in 2019. That is a simulation, not an observation, and I would not quote it as a measured error. But the direction is not in dispute, and nobody has offered me a reason it would be small.

## What the market is arguing about instead

The money is real. Indigo Ag announced its fifth issuance in February 2026: 1.1 million credits, cumulative total past two million tonnes, from more than eight million acres enrolled across 28 states since 2018, under the Climate Action Reserve's Soil Enrichment Protocol and with the ICVCM's blessing.

Meanwhile the argument that gets written about is lab versus satellite. A *Forbes* piece in June sets Groundwork BioAg, which pulls physical cores and burns them, against Boomitra, which trains machine learning on Sentinel-2 imagery and a million-odd georeferenced samples and predicts carbon without pulling a core at all. "You cannot algorithm your way out of a physical baseline," says one; the other points out that remote sensing cuts cost by over ninety percent and is the only route by which smallholders ever participate. Both have methodologies sitting side by side in the same registry. The version I read never reaches sampling depth or bulk density.

I think that argument is downstream of this one. A model trained on fixed-depth thirty-centimetre samples inherits the depth problem and the mass problem intact, and a satellite trained on those same samples inherits them with another layer of inference on top. Settling whether you measure with a shovel or a spectrometer does not touch what you are measuring.

### Where this is thinner than I have made it sound

Several of the papers that would push back hardest are ones I could not open. A 2021 *Geoderma* paper titled "No-till and carbon stocks: Is deep soil sampling necessary? Insights from long-term experiments" plainly signals an argument against everything above, and the publisher blocked me. A 2025 global synthesis in the *European Journal of Soil Science* on deep-soil carbon under alternative tillage returned a 403. A 2024 paper in *Carbon Balance and Management* on exactly the accounting-method-versus-depth question rate-limited me twice. So weight my confidence accordingly: I have read the critique's primary literature and only the titles of its best rebuttals.

The critique has soft spots of its own. Baker et al. 2007 is a commentary, not a meta-analysis, and it is nineteen years old. Those Canadian means carry standard deviations larger than themselves — 0.72 against a mean of 0.38, 0.97 against 0.23 — a reminder that deep soil carbon measurement is brutal: variance grows with depth while the signal shrinks, so "just sample deeper" may trade a known bias for unmanageable noise. Nicoloso and Rice have argued that no-till systems intensified with more biomass input do sequester carbon at depth, and I have that at abstract level only. Equivalent soil mass is not a free lunch either; there is an active literature comparing implementations of it, which suggests practitioners do not fully agree on how to do it.

---

What draws me to this is not the possibility that soil carbon credits are overstated. It is the shape of the mistake. The protocol and the physical quantity have different units; the fix has been published for decades; the standard still says thirty centimetres — because thirty centimetres is a rule an auditor can check with a tape measure, and "the rooting zone, corrected to equivalent mass" is a rule that requires somebody to understand it. Verra's October notice is a real tightening of a number chosen partly because it is easy to verify. That is usually how measurement standards get set, and usually it is fine. Here it sits directly on top of the depth where the sign of the effect changes.
