The sink holding the Antikythera mechanism's front calendar ring "was drilled through with 365 small holes, of diameter about 0.7–0.8 mm, at approximately equal spacing around the ring." That sentence comes from Yanis Bitsakis and Alexander Jones's 2016 edition of the front dial and parapegma inscriptions — the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project's own authoritative epigraphic treatment of that part of the device.

About a fifth of the calendar scale survives. Roughly eighty of the holes exist. The figure 365 is not a count of anything; it is the number of days in the Egyptian civil year the ring was taken to show, written into a physical description of the object in the grammatical form of a measurement.

In 2020 somebody measured the holes. The answer came back 354.

## What the holes are for

The front dial of the mechanism carries two concentric scales: a zodiac ring, fixed, and outside it a calendar ring bearing the twelve Egyptian months transliterated into Greek. The Egyptian civil calendar has no leap day, so it slips against the seasons by a day every four years — the "wandering year." The calendar ring was therefore made movable: it sat in a sink pierced by holes, and a peg on its back face dropped into whichever hole brought the months back into register with the sun. Reset it every few years and the dial stays honest.

Which means the hole count is not a decorative detail. It is the length of the year the ring was built to represent. Three hundred and sixty-five holes make it a solar instrument. Three hundred and fifty-four make it lunar.

Chris Budiselic, A. T. Thoeni, Michael Dubno and A. T. Ramsey went at this with a tape measure's worth of patience. Working from 50-micron micro-CT image stacks, they located the centre of each of the 81 surviving holes at least three times — 274 measurements in all, stopping when a hole's standard deviation fell to 0.05 mm or better, with the worst coming in under 0.037 mm. Mean centre-to-centre spacing: 1.365 mm, standard deviation 0.127 mm. Ring radius, fitted from 26,220 three-hole combinations within a single 37-hole surviving arc: 77.49 mm, giving a circumference of 486.90 mm.

Divide and you get the test. A 365-hole ring needs 1.334 mm spacing; a 354-hole ring needs 1.375 mm. The measured 1.365 mm sits much closer to the second. They ran a two one-sided equivalence test at 99%, treating 0.05 mm as practically zero, and found 354 the best match, counts from 354 to 359 significant, and 365 rejected with a maximum p of 0.102.

Their own paper is not tidy about this. Table 4 reports an inferred mean count of 356.7 with a 99% interval of 346.8 to 367.2 — an interval that contains 365, sitting awkwardly beside the text's rejection of it. The equivalence framing and the interval framing are answering slightly different questions, and the paper does not reconcile them. The version I worked from is the authors' preprint; a revised text appeared in the March 2021 *Horological Journal* after a digital pre-press release in December 2020.

## What Glasgow added, and what it didn't

The version that reached the press came in mid-2024 from Graham Woan and Joseph Bayley at Glasgow, both gravitational-wave people, and was reported more or less everywhere as gravitational-wave science cracking an ancient mystery.

What they did is more interesting than that framing and much less exotic. They took Budiselic's published hole coordinates and wrote down a proper generative model: N holes evenly spaced on a circle of radius r, broken into eight arc sections, six of them usable, each section free to shift and rotate — 22 unknowns in total — with Gaussian hole-position errors split into independent radial and tangential components. Uniform priors on N and r and the section placements, Jeffreys priors on the noise scales. Then they sampled it twice, with emcee and with dynesty, the second treating N as genuinely discrete, and the two agreed.

Using all the data: N = 355.24, +1.39/−1.36 at 68%, and ±3.62 at 99%. Dropping the two shortest sections and the end holes of the rest — eight holes in all, the ones adjacent to fractures where a section's inferred geometry is least trustworthy — gives N = 354.08, +1.47/−1.41. Radius 77.34 mm. And the error structure is the part I keep going back to: the radial scatter is 0.028 mm against 0.129 mm tangential, less than a quarter as large. The authors suggest some of the radial figure may be measurement noise rather than ancient workmanship — Budiselic's own quoted measurement standard deviation is 0.037 mm, which is larger than the entire radial scatter being fitted.

That last bar is the honest one. The table stops at 360, which the trimmed fit puts at about one chance in ten thousand; on the full dataset the authors make 354 some 229 times more probable than 360. And 365 appears only in the abstract, as "not plausible, given our model assumptions." On the all-data posterior, 365 sits about seven standard deviations out, which is my arithmetic rather than theirs, and is roughly why they didn't bother tabulating it.

So the hole count is now known to about ±1.4 out of 354 — better than one part in 250 — from 79 holes, which is under a quarter of the ring. That is a real result, and it is a result about geometry achieved by writing down uncertainties that the previous literature had not written down. What it is *not* is a new answer. Budiselic's group reached 354 four years earlier by a cruder route; Glasgow supplied the credible interval. The Glasgow release says as much if you read it closely — "previous studies had suggested" the lunar reading — while leading with spacetime ripples, and it introduces Budiselic once as "Antikythera researcher" and once as "YouTuber," without resolving which. The imported technique is Bayesian parameter estimation with MCMC and nested sampling, which astronomers use on gravitational waves and also on nearly everything else.

The headlines went further still. *Live Science* ran it as the world's oldest computer following the *Greek* lunar calendar. The month names engraved on the ring are Egyptian.

## The part nobody has resolved

Which brings me to the thing that made me want to write this up, and that none of the coverage touches.

The holes say 354. The engraving still says 365.

Bitsakis and Jones describe the scale as "graduated into sectors corresponding to the twelve 30-day months and the five additional 'epagomenal' days," with smaller graduations for single days, and Greek month names averaging about 1.8 mm in letter height. Three survive legibly: Pachon, Payni, Epeiph — the ninth, tenth and eleventh Egyptian months, consecutive, which is what you would expect from a single surviving arc. That is the Egyptian civil calendar, 12 × 30 + 5 = 365.

The day graduations are visible on the surviving sectors, and the edition counts them individually — the seventh through eighteenth graduation of the Pachon sector, for instance. But it counts from what it calls a presumed longer graduation marking that sector's beginning. The five-day epagomenal sector, which is the diagnostic feature of a 365-day scale, is not among the surviving material.

So: engraved Egyptian civil month names, a scale whose sector structure is partly observed and partly supplied by the interpretation, and 354 holes underneath. Budiselic's group bridge it by invoking a hypothesised later Egyptian lunar calendar that used civil month names, following Parker and Depuydt, and they are candid that this is not their field — they explicitly leave the chronology to Egyptologists. I could not find a published response from the epigraphic side; neither Jones nor Tony Freeth appears to have answered the hole count in print.

That is an unusual state for a famous object to be in. Two careful measurements of the same bronze ring now point at two different years, and the bridge between them is a hypothesis in a third discipline that neither measuring party claims competence in.

## And the gears may not have turned

The second thread arrived in April 2025, and the press mangled it in the opposite direction.

Michael Edmunds published an assessment of the mechanism's gear-train accuracy in the *Journal for the History of Astronomy* in 2011, measured tooth-position errors from the CT data, and concluded the device was probably built "more for display or education purposes than for actual astronomical calculation." Esteban Szigety and Gustavo Arenas took Edmunds's error model, combined it with Thorndike's analytical solution for the non-uniform motion that triangular teeth produce, and built a Python simulation that detects actual tooth interference rather than assuming the gears mesh.

The first finding is a tidy exoneration. Triangular teeth on their own are nearly harmless: ideal gears gave pointer scatter of 0.60° on the lunar train, 0.053° on the Metonic, 0.038° on the Saros — roughly seven times smaller than Edmunds's measured errors. Greek engineers' choice of tooth profile was not the problem.

The second finding is that the measured errors are catastrophic. With a tooth height of 1.1 mm and clearance set at the optimum 40% of tooth height — below 10% the gears jam, above 90% they disengage — a 1%-per-pair jamming criterion tolerates a random tooth error of about 0.04 and an eccentricity of about 0.1°, and Edmunds's values exceed those in most gears. Running Edmunds's numbers, 27 of 30 simulated mechanisms jammed, with mean survival times of 129 days on the lunar train, 121 on the Saros, 234 on the Metonic. The conclusion also phrases this as stopping once the solar pointer had travelled about 120°, which looks like a contradiction until you remember the solar pointer turns once a year: 120° is about 122 days, and the two statements agree.

Then the authors' own verdict: either the mechanism never worked, or its real errors were smaller than Edmunds measured. And they argue for the second, on grounds that seem to me obviously right. The gears spent two thousand years in seawater. Many are partly destroyed. CT resolution may be insufficient to place tooth tips and valleys. Edmunds was measuring corrosion plus deformation plus whatever the craftsman did, with no way to separate the three.

*ScienceAlert* ran this as "Mysterious Antikythera Mechanism May Actually Be a Toy." *Gizmodo* called the device a mechanical disaster; *artnet* reported that it "didn't function very well." The word toy does not appear as the authors' suggestion anywhere in the paper — the nearest thing in the literature is Edmunds's own "display or education," from 2011, about which the 2025 authors are arguing the other way. *Live Science*'s headline asked whether it was a "janky toy" while its body correctly reported both the corrosion caveat and that the researchers reject the toy reading, which is a strange document to be.

## What the fragment can and cannot say

What I take from a day inside this is narrower than either headline and, I think, more useful.

Everything anyone knows about this object is a measurement of a corroded fragment, and as the measurements have got better they have stopped agreeing with each other. The 365 in the standard inscription edition is a reconstruction in the grammar of an observation. The 354 is a genuine inference, pinned to 0.4%, from under a quarter of a ring, against engraving that still reads solar. The gear errors are real numbers attached to an unanswerable question about what produced them, and the most defensible thing in the 2025 paper is not its simulation but its refusal to treat Edmunds's figures as the original tolerances.

The thing that actually improved in the last six years is not knowledge of the mechanism. It is that three separate groups have now written down what they don't know in a form somebody else can check — an interval instead of a figure, a tolerance instead of a verdict, a hypothesis handed to the discipline that could test it. The device is no more legible than it was. The error bars are.
