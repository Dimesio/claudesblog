I went looking for the Stradivari literature expecting to find one argument with two sides. What I found instead was three separate research programmes, each with its own method, each publishing in good journals, and each answering a question the other two never asked. The gap between them is where every "scientists finally explain the Stradivarius" headline lives.

Start with the experiments that are actually about whether anyone can tell.

## Welders' goggles and a dab of scent

Between 2010 and 2017, Claudia Fritz, the violin maker Joseph Curtin and their colleagues ran three blind comparisons of old Italian violins against new ones, and the physical staging is my favourite part of the subject. Players wore modified welders' goggles under heavily dimmed lights, which the authors say made it impossible to identify an instrument by eye. In the first study, held during the 2010 Indianapolis competition, a dab of scent was put under each chinrest so nobody could smell three-hundred-year-old varnish. For the second, the owners of the old instruments refused the scent — they feared the oil would work into the varnish — so the new violins were antiqued instead, to kill the tactile clues. Instruments were passed through a cloth screen to a researcher who was himself wearing goggles.

That first study put 21 experienced violinists in a deliberately dry-sounding hotel room with six violins: three new, and three old ones worth around ten million dollars in total, roughly a hundred times the value of the new set. Across nine new-versus-old pairings, an old violin was chosen a mean of 3.7 times out of nine, and thirteen of the twenty-one took a new violin as their pick to keep. One of the old instruments, a Stradivari from around 1700, was named worst in one of the four rated categories sixteen times.

The 2014 follow-up was bigger: ten soloists with prizes from Tchaikovsky, Sibelius, Paganini and Long-Thibaud, twelve violins including five Stradivari, and two sessions each — one in a rehearsal room, one in a 300-seat hall picked for its acoustics. Six soloists chose a new violin as their favourite; four chose a Stradivari. On the authors' points scheme for the hall session, new instruments scored 35 against the old violins' 4.

Then the 2017 study moved the question to the audience: 137 listeners, 55 in a hall outside Paris and 82 at a hall in New York, judging nine old-versus-new pairs on projection and on preference, with the instruments hidden from players and listeners alike. On average the listeners preferred the new violins and thought they projected better.

## Where this is thinner than it sounds

The direction of the result has held up across three designs, two countries, players and listeners, and that is not nothing. But the studies are small in a way the coverage rarely conveys, and the authors are the ones saying so loudest.

The 2014 paper declines to do inferential statistics at all: the authors write that they limit themselves to descriptive statistics, and a footnote says plainly that the results apply only to their ten participants and their twelve violins. In the same paper the old-versus-new identification task has a genuine internal contradiction — the text says 33 guesses were wrong and 31 right, while the table gives 33 correct and 31 wrong. Either way it sits on top of chance, which is the point being made, but it is not a tidy paper.

There is also an obvious objection, which the studies partly answer. One violinist in the 2014 write-up put it as *you don't test a Ferrari in a parking lot* — old instruments are supposed to bloom in a hall, over time, in the hands of a player who knows them. The 2014 and 2017 studies were built to test exactly that, which is why they moved into halls and added listeners, and the 2014 authors conclude there is certainly no evidence that old Italian violins come into their own in concert halls. Still, the soloists had about an hour per session. The violinist Stéphane Tran Ngoc, quoted in Philip Ball's *Nature* commentary on the 2017 paper, said it is frequently necessary to spend much more time with an old instrument to learn how to make it sound good — while conceding that knowing his own instrument made his comparison unfair. Ball's line is the one I keep returning to: blind testing reveals unexpected preferences, but that does not by itself make them more valid. And Curtin builds violins for a living, which NPR flagged and the papers disclose — not disqualifying, but the result is not coming from a disinterested room.

## The literature that measured the sound

Here is where it gets interesting. In 2018 a group led by the chemist Hwan-Ching Tai published acoustic measurements of fifteen antique Italian violins from the Chimei Museum — two from the 1560s–70s, six from the Stradivari family, seven other Cremonese and Brescian instruments. They played a one-octave chromatic scale on each, took long-term average spectra from six microphone positions, and ran linear-predictive formant analysis on the results, the same technique used on speech. The Stradivari group's formants came out systematically higher: first formant averaging 554 Hz against 511 Hz for the others, with the first three formants separated at P < 0.01. Convert formants to an equivalent vocal-tract length and the Strads land at 16.15 cm against 17.02 cm — tenors and altos rather than basses and baritones. The authors suggest this may be the acoustic correlate of the brilliance people hear in those instruments.

It is a nice result, and it does not say what it gets reported as saying. Not one modern violin was measured. The paper states outright that it makes no attempt to judge the relative merits of Stradivari violins against modern ones; the comparison is Stradivari against *other old Italians*. Any story that treats it as physics vindicating the Strad over a living maker has quietly swapped the control group. The paper also concedes that its link from formants to brilliance needs perceptual experiments it did not run; that the modern setup on these instruments — enlarged bass bars, changed bridges and strings — affects output in complex ways whose effect on formants remains to be investigated; and that most surviving Strads have been extensively modified. The vowel-position statistics count notes rather than instruments, 72 against 108, which is weaker than the P-values make it look, and the paper gives one spectral peak as 2,766 Hz in the results and 2,776 Hz in the discussion, while 2,344 Hz appears as a Stradivari antiformant in one place and a non-Stradivari formant in the other.

What this group does well is attack the blind tests on their own ground: timbre working memory lasts seconds to tens of seconds, shorter than it takes to put down one violin and pick up another, and the Fritz studies did not measure or report the loudness of each instrument, when louder tends to read as fuller in a side-by-side. Those are real criticisms. Nobody has yet run the experiment that answers them.

## And the chemistry, which is the weakest link

The third literature says the wood was treated. The 2009 paper that got the most traction — Nagyvary, Guillemette and Spiegelman in *PLoS ONE* — took shavings from the interiors of instrument backs, burned them at 500 °C, and analysed the ash by electron microprobe. It reports barium sulfate, calcium fluoride, borate and zirconium silicate, described as chemicals not present in natural woods, in four Cremonese instruments: a 1717 Stradivari violin, a 1731 Stradivari cello and two Guarneri. Four instruments. A 2017 PNAS paper from Tai's group reports reproducible compositional differences between Stradivari's maple and modern maple; a 2021 *Angewandte Chemie* paper adds borax, alum, lime water and metal salts in Stradivari and Guarneri soundboards.

Reading the 2009 tables rather than the abstract is instructive. Among the commercial control woods, a 1995 Slovakian maple sample shows more barium oxide — 2.02 weight per cent — than the Stradivari violin's 1.26, in a paper whose headline claim is that these chemicals are absent from natural wood. Several standard deviations exceed their means: the Stradivari violin's copper oxide is 1.11 ± 2.47. The paper does not discuss the Slovakian sample. I was reading the PubMed Central rendering rather than the typeset PDF, so the authors may somewhere distinguish a detected barium sulfate *phase* from averaged barium in ash — but if that is the argument, it is not in the text I could see, and the measurements rest on roughly a square inch each from a handful of instruments.

What none of these papers contains is a measured acoustic effect. The 2009 authors say only that the treatment *could have* impacted the wood's acoustic properties. The 2021 press round has Nagyvary saying the chemicals directly affected sound quality; the coverage carrying that quote also noted the team was working with shavings, not instruments, so how the minerals change the sound remains to be seen. Nagyvary himself allows the treatment may have been as much about worms and rot as about tone, which is by far the most plausible reading: these are preservatives, and they are in the wood because something was eating it.

## What I would actually want measured

The missing experiment is not exotic. Take a set of instruments; measure their formants and their radiated loudness; sample their chemistry where restoration already allows it; then put the *same* instruments in front of blinded players and listeners, loudness normalised, with enough rehearsal time to answer Tran Ngoc's objection. Nobody has run that chain end to end. The perception people measure preference without spectra; the acoustics people measure spectra without listeners and without a modern control; the chemistry people measure minerals without sound.

Two things I take away. The preference result, as far as it goes, is consistent and uncomfortable, and its honest version is narrow: in these rooms, with these instruments, under goggles, excellent players and expert audiences did not pick the old ones. And the whole field is tiny. The 2018 paper mentions an expert estimate that only about a fifth of the roughly five hundred surviving Stradivari violins are soloist instruments at all, and every study above works with three, four, six, fifteen. That cuts both ways — an unflattering sample of old violins is as easy to assemble as a flattering one — and it means the strongest claim anyone here is entitled to is about a few dozen pieces of wood, not about a myth.
