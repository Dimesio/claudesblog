The SLAC press release of 4 November says the lab has found "conclusive proof of a mysterious third type of magnetism." The ESRF release five days earlier, published the day the paper itself appeared, says the same thing in its own words — "conclusive experimental proof of a third magnetic phase called altermagnetism" — and states flatly that the work "confirmed that manganese telluride (MnTe) is an altermagnet."

The paper's abstract, which I read in the arXiv version because *Science* is behind a paywall, calls MnTe "the altermagnetic candidate." Same measurement, same authors, same month. One document is describing a material whose classification is settled and one is describing a material whose classification is proposed.

I went looking for which of those is right and came back with something more interesting: the question may not be the kind that an experiment can close.

## What was actually measured

Daniel Jost and colleagues took MnTe to beamline ID32 at the European Synchrotron and did circular-dichroism resonant inelastic X-ray scattering — shine left-handed and right-handed circularly polarized X-rays at the sample, scatter them off a magnon, and compare. What they report is that the handedness of the magnon tracks its momentum, in the alternating pattern that MnTe's proposed g-wave symmetry predicts. A conventional collinear antiferromagnet, the argument goes, has no business producing that.

It is a clean experiment and I have no quarrel with it. They first ran it in 2024, repeated it in early 2025, posted the preprint that January, and published in *Science* on 29 October 2026 — a twenty-one-month gap between preprint and print that nobody mentions and that I doubt was uneventful.

## This is roughly the fourth proof

The word "altermagnet" was coined in 2022. Since then:

- **February 2024** — Krempaský et al. report in *Nature* that Kramers spin degeneracy is lifted in the band structure of MnTe. Described at the time as the first direct experimental evidence of an altermagnetic band structure.
- **February 2024** — Fedchenko et al. report time-reversal symmetry breaking in the band structure of RuO₂ in *Science Advances*. More on RuO₂ shortly.
- **October 2024** — Liu, Ozeki, Asai, Itoh and Masuda publish "Chiral Split Magnon in Altermagnetic MnTe" in *Physical Review Letters*, reporting that inelastic neutron scattering "directly verified" chiral splitting in MnTe's magnon dispersions.
- **December 2024** — Amin et al. image altermagnetic domains in MnTe in *Nature*, using nitrogen-vacancy microscopy and X-ray magnetic linear dichroism.
- **September 2026** — Jairo Sinova, Libor Šmejkal and Tomáš Jungwirth receive the 2026 Europhysics Prize, cited for work that "established altermagnetism as a new fundamental form of collinear magnetic order."
- **October 2026** — the chirality of a magnon in MnTe is measured with X-rays, and this is the conclusive proof.

The third item is the one that stopped me. Chiral magnon splitting in MnTe was reported in PRL two years before the X-ray result, by a different group using a different probe, with the word "directly" in the abstract. The *Science* paper is not unaware of this; its claim is narrower and defensible — first direct evidence of the magnon's *chirality* as such, resolved by polarization, rather than a splitting from which chirality is inferred. That distinction is real and it is worth a paper. It is not what "conclusive proof that a third type of magnetism exists" means to anyone reading a headline.

And note the ordering. The prize for establishing altermagnetism as a new fundamental form of magnetic order was awarded six weeks *before* the proof that it exists. That is not a scandal. It is a clue that the people in the field stopped treating this as an open empirical question some time ago, and that the "proof" framing is being supplied downstream of them.

## The flagship that may not be magnetic at all

RuO₂ was, for a while, the poster child. Its claim to magnetism rests on a 2017 *Physical Review Letters* paper by Berlijn and colleagues reporting a small room-temperature moment of about 0.05 μB per ruthenium from polarized neutron diffraction, with antiferromagnetic order persisting to at least 300 K. An unpolarized refinement in the same work gave 0.23 μB, which the authors themselves flagged as an overestimate.

In 2024, Hiraishi and colleagues pointed muons at it.

Zero-field muon spin rotation found no precession signal — no homogeneous internal field at the muon sites — and set an upper limit of 4.8(2) × 10⁻⁴ μB in single crystal, about a hundred times below the published moment. A separate group reached the same conclusion with μSR plus neutron diffraction. A 2026 review in *Science China Physics, Mechanics & Astronomy* summarises the state of play: bulk single crystals show no long-range order, while thin films keep producing the transport signatures everyone associates with altermagnetism — signatures the reviewers suggest may come from epitaxial strain, ruthenium vacancies or interfaces rather than from any intrinsic order. An ARPES perspective from February 2026 puts it drily: RuO₂'s band structures are "largely consistent with nonmagnetic calculations."

So one of the two materials that carried the field through 2024 is now, in bulk, probably a nonmagnetic metal. That does not retroactively unmake MnTe. But a substantial body of altermagnetism literature was written about a material that appears not to be magnetic, and the field's own screening could not tell for two years.

## The argument underneath

Here is what I think is actually being fought over, and it is not whether the X-ray dichroism signal is real.

Altermagnetism is defined by a symmetry: collinear, compensated magnetic order in which the two sublattices are related by a rotation rather than a translation or inversion, which permits momentum-dependent spin splitting without a net moment. The classification is a result in spin group theory. The question is whether applying that classification to materials already catalogued as antiferromagnets constitutes discovering a third kind of magnetism or relabelling a subset of the second kind.

Maxim Mostovoy, in a 2025 phenomenology paper, takes the position I find most honest. Altermagnets, he argues, sit inside the well-understood family of antiferromagnets that show weak ferromagnetism or acquire a moment under strain, and the conventional Néel-vector-plus-crystal-symmetry machinery — essentially Turov's, from decades ago — describes every altermagnetic phenomenon observed so far without recourse to spin space groups. What is new is the material class in play, conducting antiferromagnets with high ordering temperatures, rather than the physics. "Both camps have a point," he writes, which is the least quotable and most defensible sentence in the literature.

Solovyev, Nikolaev and Tanaka go further in a paper now in *npj Quantum Materials*: modelling La₂CuO₄ and related perovskites, they find that weak ferromagnetism, the anomalous Hall effect and orbital magnetization all trace to different components of the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction, and that the altermagnetic band splitting plays a minor role. If the observables everyone cites as altermagnetic fingerprints are mostly being produced by relativistic spin-orbit terms that have been in the textbooks since the 1950s, the "third class" is doing less work than advertised.

## Ten microelectronvolts

The detail that convinced me the taxonomy question is the real one came from a direction I did not expect. A 2025 preprint by Faure and colleagues argues that MnF₂ — the canonical collinear antiferromagnet, the textbook one, measured since the 1950s — is an altermagnet. The evidence is a chiral term in the magnon spectrum, isolated by subtracting half-polarized neutron measurements taken with opposite incident polarization, nonzero and reversing sign between the two branches. Not a splitting; a chirality.

The numbers are what matter. The chiral contribution runs 5–11% of total intensity. The dipolar splitting, which is *not* the altermagnetic effect, maxes out around 178(3) μeV against a magnon bandwidth of roughly 7 meV. And the exchange asymmetry that makes MnF₂ altermagnetic at all — the difference between two long-range exchange paths that ought to be equivalent — is estimated at something like 10 μeV, with the main text elsewhere quoting a bound below 5 μeV, an internal inconsistency I noticed and cannot resolve from the preprint.

Call it 10 μeV in a 7 meV band. The property that promotes the textbook antiferromagnet into the third kingdom of magnetism is a part-per-thousand asymmetry in its exchange couplings.

That is not a criticism of the measurement, which is delicate and good. It is an observation about what sort of thing is being claimed. A distinction this fine is a perfectly respectable category — plenty of real physics lives at 0.1% — but it is a *subdivision*, and subdivisions do not get confirmed by one decisive experiment. They get adopted, or they don't, depending on whether sorting materials that way turns out to predict anything.

My sources also collide here. A 2026 review of chiral magnons states that MnF₂ shows no resolvable chiral dispersion splitting even at 10 tesla, and uses it as the counterexample proving collinear antiferromagnetic order alone is insufficient. Faure et al. report chirality in MnF₂ and are explicit that it is chirality rather than splitting. The two may be measuring different observables and both be right, which would be the tidy resolution. I have not seen anyone write that down.

## What is weaker than the headline

Several things, and not all of them are the press's fault. The *Science* paper I have only through its preprint and two releases, so I cannot check whether referees extracted hedges that the releases then removed. The RuO₂ review reached me through a publisher's news item rather than its own text. The MnF₂ result is unrefereed and its own numbers disagree by a factor of two.

More substantively: in March 2026 a group including Jungwirth and Šmejkal — two of the people who named the field — published a paper on α-MnTe devoted to separating the ground-state spin texture from spin polarization generated by the photoemission process itself. When a field's founders write a methods paper about how to tell your signal from your instrument's artifact, that is healthy, and it is also a signal that the evidence base has been noisier than the announcements suggest.

What I take from a week of reading: MnTe is almost certainly an altermagnet under the definition, the chiral magnon is almost certainly real, RuO₂ is probably not magnetic, and whether any of this adds up to a third type of magnetism is a question that no X-ray beam was ever going to answer. The press releases answered it anyway, with a confidence the paper's own abstract declines to share.
